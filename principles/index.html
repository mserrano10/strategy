1. What is (Good) Investing?

Quote[cite: 2]: > “Investing is often described as the process of laying out money now in the expectation of receiving more money in the future[cite: 2]. At Berkshire we take a more demanding approach, defining investing as the transfer to others of purchasing power now with the reasoned expectation of receiving more purchasing power – after taxes have been paid on nominal gains – in the future[cite: 2]. More succinctly, investing is forgoing consumption now in order to have the ability to consume more at a later date.”[cite: 2] >
— Warren E. Buffett[cite: 2]

Stealing also from the chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, a simple example illustrates how investment should be evaluated[cite: 2]:

  • If you forgo today 10 hamburgers to purchase an investment[cite: 2];
  • Then receive dividends which, after tax, buy you 2 hamburgers[cite: 2]; and
  • After that receive, upon sale of your holdings, after-tax proceeds that will buy you eight more hamburgers[cite: 2];
  • Then you have had no real income from your investment, no matter how much it appreciated in dollars[cite: 2]. You may feel richer, but you won't be richer[cite: 2].

In financial terms, what matters is your after-tax, inflation-adjusted returns[cite: 2]. This return is best measured by the Compounded Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)—not the average annual return nor deceitful "risk-adjusted returns."[cite: 2]

2. How Purchasing Power is Built

When you can acquire a (1) quality business at a (2) favorable price, led by (3) able management, you position yourself to reap substantial returns[cite: 2].

Three Asset Categories

  1. Currency-Based Investments: Money-market funds, bonds, mortgages, CDs[cite: 2]. Often mistakenly deemed safe, their purchasing power deteriorates due to continuous monetary expansion[cite: 2].
  2. Unproductive Assets: Gold, silver, and precious metals[cite: 2]. These rely entirely on speculative demand rather than cash generation[cite: 2].
  3. Productive Assets: Businesses, farms, or select real estate[cite: 2]. These maintain output purchasing power through economic cycles requiring minimal incremental capital[cite: 2].
Core Insight

A low-cost index fund of the S&P 500 will outperform most strategies over time[cite: 2]. Owning exceptional individual businesses can outperform the whole when executed within a strict, value-driven framework[cite: 2].

3. Price & Intrinsic Value

Quote[cite: 2]: > “It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price.”[cite: 2] >
— Charlie Munger[cite: 2]

Opportunity cost dictates what you should pay today for future cash flow[cite: 2]. Consider Apple in 2023[cite: 2]: generating $99.6B in Free Cash Flow against a $2.99T market cap[cite: 2]:

$$\text{FCF Yield} = \frac{\$99.6\text{B}}{\$2.99\text{T}} \approx 3.33\%$$[cite: 2]

If 1-year U.S. Treasuries yield 5%, holding Apple required believing its long-term growth would offset the immediate 1.67% yield deficit[cite: 2].

Valuation Heuristics
  • Welcome lower prices in owned businesses to acquire more at better yields[cite: 2].
  • Ignore short-term price forecasts; focus strictly on business operations[cite: 2].
  • Market prices and intrinsic values diverge frequently but eventually converge[cite: 2].

4. Time Horizon & Risk

Quote[cite: 2]: > “In the stock market, the most important organ is the stomach. Not the brain.”[cite: 2] >
— Peter Lynch[cite: 2]
  • Evaluate business performance over 5 to 10-year test windows rather than single-year quarters[cite: 2].
  • Volatility is not synonymous with risk[cite: 2]. Real risk is the permanent loss of purchasing power[cite: 2].
  • Widespread market fear provides bargain entry points; personal emotional fear is the primary hazard[cite: 2].

5. Guiding Axioms

  • Monetary intervention distorts interest rates and artificially prices time[cite: 2].
  • Portfolios must endure periodic net-worth drawdowns of ~50% during structural bust cycles[cite: 2].
  • Long-term historical market returns (~7%–11% CAGR) benefit those who avoid forced liquidations during economic panics[cite: 2].
“Time in the market beats timing the market.”[cite: 2]
